## What Is Outer Knee Pain?

**Outer knee pain**, **pain exterior knee**, and **pain on the outside of the knee** are used interchangeably. They all describe discomfort on the lateral (outer) side of the knee, where the thigh bone, shin bone, outer meniscus, outer ligament, and the IT band meet. It may show up with walking, running, stairs, squatting, twisting, after exercise, or even at rest.

Because several tissues share this small area, location alone cannot tell you the cause. The sections below explain the most common ones and how to tell when to try conservative care or get checked.

## Common Causes of Pain on the Outside of the Knee

### IT Band-Related Pain

The iliotibial (IT) band is a thick band of connective tissue running from the hip to the outer knee. Repeated knee bending, as in running, cycling, and hiking, can irritate the area near the outer knee. Pain is often described as sharp or burning on the outside of the knee that appears after a certain distance and eases with rest.

### Lateral Meniscus Injury

The meniscus is a cushion in the knee. A twist, deep squat, or sudden pivot can injure the outer meniscus. Symptoms may include sharp pain on the outside of the knee, catching, a feeling of locking, swelling, and pain with twisting. These symptoms warrant an evaluation.

### LCL Injury

The lateral collateral ligament (LCL) stabilizes the outer knee. A sprain usually follows a blow to the inner knee or a twisting injury and may cause outer knee tenderness, swelling, and a feeling of looseness.

### Patellofemoral Factors

Pain around the kneecap can be felt toward the outer side, especially with stairs, squatting, and prolonged sitting. See [runner's knee](/health/runners-knee).

### Overuse and Training Changes

Rapid increases in mileage, new hill work, a change of shoes, or longer shifts on your feet commonly precede pain on the outside of the knee.

### Referred Pain From the Hip or Low Back

Weak or tight hip muscles and irritation of nerves from the low back can contribute to outer knee symptoms. Read about [hip pain](/health/hip-pain) and [sciatica](/health/sciatica).

## Symptom Modifiers: When Does It Hurt?

| When it hurts | Possible contributors |
| --- | --- |
| Walking | Gait mechanics, IT band irritation, ligament or meniscus issues |
| Running | Training load, IT band region, hip control, footwear |
| Stairs (especially going down) | Patellofemoral and IT band factors |
| Squatting or kneeling | Meniscus, kneecap, deep-bend loading |
| Twisting or pivoting | Meniscus, ligament |
| After exercise | Overuse, load tolerance |
| At rest or at night | Inflammation, more significant injury; get evaluated if persistent |

## Conservative Care vs. Getting Evaluated Promptly

**You can reasonably try conservative care for a short period if:**

- Pain is mild to moderate and linked to a recent activity increase
- You can walk normally and bear weight
- There is little or no swelling
- There was no significant injury
- Pain eases when you reduce the aggravating activity

Conservative steps include temporarily reducing aggravating volume, avoiding sharp-pain activities, keeping pain-free movement, and gradually building back.

**Get an evaluation promptly if:**

- The knee locks, catches, or gives way
- There is major swelling or visible deformity
- You cannot bear weight
- Pain started after a fall, twist, or collision
- You have fever, redness, or warmth around the joint
- Pain is severe at night or does not improve after a couple of weeks

## Treatment Pathway

1. **Evaluation:** history, knee tests, hip, thigh, ankle, and foot review.
2. **Mobility:** restoring motion where hips, ankles, or the knee are restricted.
3. **Load modification:** adjusting mileage, hills, and sport volume so tissues can recover.
4. **Strengthening:** hip, thigh, and calf strength to improve how the knee is loaded.
5. **Gait and movement assessment:** running, stairs, and squat mechanics.
6. **Sport-specific return:** a graded plan to resume running, cycling, hiking, or work duties.

## The Hip-to-Foot Connection

The knee sits between the hip and foot. Reduced hip control, thigh fatigue, limited ankle mobility, footwear, and training surface can change how force passes through the outside of the knee. Muscles around the hip and thigh may become overworked or guarded, particularly after earlier injury.

Seek urgent care for a knee that is deformed, cannot bear weight, has rapid swelling after injury, or comes with fever, a hot red joint, or calf swelling with shortness of breath.
