## The short answer

**Robotic Precision Therapy (RPT) is a clinician-directed treatment that uses the RX2600 Therapeutic Robot to apply controlled, repeatable pressure to muscles.** If you are reading reviews, this page covers what they tend to be about: what the treatment is, what is good and hard about it, what it costs, and how to decide.

It does not replace the experience of a patient. Individual experiences vary, and no review can predict your result. The goal here is to help you ask better questions and decide whether an evaluation is worth your time.

## What RPT is, in plain language

A clinician assesses how you move and where muscles are tight or guarded. If RPT appears appropriate, the RX2600 applies pressure to the areas the clinician selects, and the pressure can be adjusted during the session. It does not diagnose a condition. For the full explanation, read [what is Robotic Precision Therapy](/health/what-is-robotic-precision-therapy).

## What people tend to like

- **A full-length first visit.** The first appointment is 90 minutes so there is time for an assessment and a treatment.
- **Clear pricing.** The price is listed, rather than depending on a plan's allowed amount.
- **Consistent pressure.** The robot applies pressure that can be set and repeated.
- **A focus on muscles.** It looks at muscular contributors to pain, such as tight hips or back muscles, that people may not have had addressed.

## What people tend to find hard

- **It is out-of-pocket.** You pay at the visit and may seek reimbursement afterward.
- **Firm pressure.** Treatment can be uncomfortable over sore areas.
- **Uncertainty about visits.** It is normal to want a number, but it depends on your condition and response.
- **It is not a diagnosis.** If your pain has another cause, you may need a physician.

## Who may want to ask about RPT

- People with back, neck, shoulder, hip, or leg pain that keeps returning
- People with stiffness after an injury has otherwise settled
- People who want longer, more focused treatment time than a short insurance-limited visit
- People who are comfortable with out-of-pocket care

If that sounds like you, the guides on [back pain](/health/back-pain), [hip pain](/health/hip-pain), and [sciatica](/health/sciatica) explain how those problems are commonly approached.

## Who should see a physician first

RPT is not an emergency or diagnostic service. See a physician first if you have:

- Severe pain after a fall or accident
- Loss of bladder or bowel control, or groin or saddle numbness
- New weakness in an arm or leg
- Fever with severe pain, or a painful, swollen leg
- Chest pain or shortness of breath

## How to read reviews of any clinic

1. **Look for specifics.** The problem, the process, the timeline, and how staff communicated.
2. **Read the critical ones.** Complaints about scheduling, cost, or expectations are useful.
3. **Prefer verified platforms.** Independent sites where reviewers are verified are more informative than selected testimonials.
4. **Match the problem.** A review about a different condition says less about yours.
5. **Treat guarantees as a warning.** "Cured in one visit" is not a reliable claim.

Reviews cannot tell you whether your pain has the same cause as the reviewer's. Only an assessment can address that.

## Questions to ask before you book

- Who will assess me, and who will operate the equipment?
- What will you do if my symptoms look like they need a physician?
- What does the first visit include and cost?
- How will we know whether it is helping, and when would we stop?
- What paperwork can you give me for reimbursement or HSA/FSA use?
