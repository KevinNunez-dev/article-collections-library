## Still in pain after trying the usual options?

If you have tried physical therapy, chiropractic care, massage, pain creams, stretching, or medication and still hurt, it is reasonable to ask what you might be missing. The answer is not always another treatment. It may be a fresh assessment, a clearer diagnosis, a different plan, or a conversation about whether a particular muscle is contributing to your symptoms.

The **RX2600 Therapeutic Robot** is used in Robotic Precision Therapy (RPT), a clinician-directed approach that applies controlled, repeatable pressure to selected muscles. RPT Clinic in Troy, Michigan, offers this treatment with therapists trained in the RPT approach. It may be an option to ask about when muscle tension is part of the picture, but it is not a cure-all, a substitute for medical diagnosis, or a proven replacement for physical therapy, chiropractic care, or other appropriate treatment.

The most useful question is not simply, “Which treatment is best?” It is: **What is driving my symptoms, what does this option aim to address, and how will we know whether it is helping?**

## What “muscle imbalance” can—and cannot—tell you

People use “muscle imbalance” to describe differences in strength, flexibility, coordination, or tension between muscles. For example, an area may feel tight while another feels weak or hard to activate. Those patterns can affect movement, but the phrase is broad. It does not identify a specific disease, prove the cause of pain, or show that one muscle is responsible for a complicated symptom.

Pain can involve muscles, joints, nerves, tendons, discs, prior injuries, general health, stress, sleep, and more than one factor at the same time. A sore or tight muscle may be part of the problem—or a response to another problem. Even imaging findings such as a herniated disc need to be interpreted alongside symptoms and an examination; a scan alone does not determine which treatment is right.

An assessment should connect the findings to your experience: where it hurts, what movements change it, whether symptoms travel, what limits your day, and what you have already tried. If the explanation is only “your muscles are out of balance,” ask what that means in your case and how the plan will address a goal you can notice, such as walking farther, sleeping better, or returning to an activity.

## How common pain-relief options differ

No single approach is right for everyone. Consider the purpose of each option rather than treating “natural,” “hands-on,” or “high-tech” as a guarantee of effectiveness.

### Physical therapy

Physical therapy is a broad form of care. Depending on your needs, a plan may include an evaluation, education, movement practice, strengthening, mobility work, and ways to manage symptoms during daily activities. Ask how the exercises relate to your goals, how to adjust them if symptoms change, and how progress will be measured.

If you have already tried PT without the result you wanted, that does not automatically mean physical therapy cannot help. The diagnosis, goals, treatment plan, visit frequency, home practice, and time available may all matter. You can ask for a progress review, a revised plan, or a referral for another medical opinion. For a comparison of PT and RPT, see [physical therapy vs. Robotic Precision Therapy](/health/physical-therapy-vs-robotic-precision-therapy-lower-back-pain).

### Chiropractic care and the question of a herniated disc

Chiropractic care can involve spinal manipulation and other approaches. Whether manipulation is appropriate depends on the person, the symptoms, and the clinical assessment—not just the words “herniated disc.” A disc can be present without causing symptoms; when pain travels into an arm or leg, a clinician may need to evaluate possible nerve involvement.

The American College of Physicians guideline discusses spinal manipulation among non-drug options for some nonradicular low-back pain. It also says evidence was insufficient or lacking to determine treatments for radicular low-back pain. That distinction matters: guidance for general low-back pain should not be read as proof that manipulation treats a herniated disc or is right for every person with radiating pain. Ask the clinician to explain the expected benefit, risks, alternatives, and what symptoms would mean you should stop or seek medical care.

### Massage and personal massage tools

Massage may feel soothing and may help some people manage muscle tension or discomfort. A massage gun, foam roller, or personal massager can also provide temporary sensory relief for some users. These tools do not establish why pain is happening, and more pressure is not necessarily better. Avoid pressing directly on an area with a suspected injury, significant swelling, unexplained numbness, or sharp worsening pain; ask a clinician what is appropriate for your situation.

Massage and devices for home use may complement a broader plan, but they are not the same as a clinical evaluation. If relief lasts only briefly or symptoms keep returning, discuss what other factors or goals should be considered.

### Ointments, creams, and other topical products

Topical products vary: some create cooling or warming sensations, while others contain medication. Their effects and precautions depend on the ingredients and the person using them. Read the label, follow the directions, and ask a pharmacist or clinician if you have questions about allergies, other medications, pregnancy, or how a product interacts with your health conditions.

An ointment may help with a symptom for a time, but it does not tell you what is causing the pain. The ACP low-back-pain guideline does not evaluate topical therapies, so it should not be used to claim that a particular cream works—or does not work—for your condition.

### Medication, injections, and other care

Medication decisions depend on your health history, the type of pain, and potential benefits and harms. Injections and procedures have specific indications and risks. Discuss these choices with the clinician who knows your situation, and do not stop a prescribed treatment or delay a needed evaluation based on an advertisement or a single testimonial.

For ongoing pain, different approaches may be combined. A plan should explain why each part is included, what it is expected to do, and when it will be reviewed.

## If a treatment has not helped, revisit the plan

“I tried physical therapy” or “I tried massage” can describe very different experiences. Before deciding that an entire type of care has failed, review what was assessed, what the treatment aimed to change, how long it was tried, and whether the plan changed when your response was limited. A useful follow-up conversation is specific: “I can sit for 20 minutes but not an hour,” or “the pain is less intense, but I still cannot lift comfortably.”

Track a few meaningful activities rather than judging progress only by how you feel at one moment. Note what you can do, what triggers symptoms, how long relief lasts, and any new symptoms. Share that information with your clinician. If you are not progressing, ask whether the diagnosis, goals, treatment dose, or next step should be reconsidered. Depending on your symptoms, that may mean adjusting the plan, seeking a medical evaluation, or considering a different approach—not simply repeating visits indefinitely.

## What the RX2600 Therapeutic Robot does

RPT Clinic describes the RX2600 as a tool used during Robotic Precision Therapy to apply controlled, repeatable pressure to specific muscles. The clinician assesses the person, selects the area and treatment approach, and remains responsible for directing the session. The robot is not an autonomous diagnostic system, does not perform surgery, and should not be confused with a robot that operates on the spine.

An Hour Detroit article published in 2022 describes the RX2600 as administering gentle pressure in intervals to a specific muscle. That piece is labeled **Sponsored Content** and was published on behalf of Robotic Precision Therapy Clinic. It is useful for understanding how the clinic presented the service; it is not independent clinical research establishing effectiveness, safety for every person, or superiority over other treatments.

The distinction is important. A device can make the delivery of pressure consistent, but that alone does not prove that the selected muscle is the main cause of your pain or that a treatment will change your condition. Ask what the assessment found, why the clinician chose a target area, how pressure can be adjusted, and what will count as meaningful progress.

RPT should also be compared with the whole care plan, not just with hands-on pressure from another provider. Ask whether movement, exercise, education, or follow-up with another clinician may also be appropriate for your goals. If a treatment is proposed as the only answer for a disc problem, nerve symptoms, or a complex condition, ask how that conclusion was reached and what would prompt a referral. A clear answer includes what the approach is intended to address, what it is not intended to address, and how the clinician will respond if you do not improve.

## Who might ask about RPT—and who should ask more first

You might ask about RPT if a clinician has assessed you and believes muscle tension is a relevant contributor to your symptoms, or if you want to understand a device-assisted pressure approach alongside other options. A visit should leave room for questions and for the clinician to say when RPT is not a fit.

It may not be the right first step if your symptoms need medical evaluation, if you have a new injury or unexplained changes, or if a provider is promising to fix a disc, release a pinched nerve, or permanently correct an imbalance. Treatment should be planned around your health history, current symptoms, comfort, and goals. ## Questions to ask before choosing any treatment

- What is your working explanation for my symptoms, and what else could cause them?
- What will this treatment try to change: pain, movement, strength, or another goal?
- What is the evidence for this option in a situation like mine?
- What are the risks, costs, and reasonable alternatives?
- How will we track progress, and when will we reconsider the plan if I am not improving?
- Could my symptoms require a physician, imaging, or another specialist before treatment?

These questions are useful whether you are comparing physical therapy, a chiropractor, massage, an ointment, or a new treatment device. Be cautious about any clinic or product that guarantees a cure, tells you to ignore medical advice, pressures you to buy a large package before an evaluation, or cannot explain what would make it stop or change the plan.

## When to seek medical care promptly

Get urgent medical help for new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the groin or saddle area, or rapidly worsening weakness in an arm or leg. Seek prompt medical assessment for severe pain after an accident, fever with significant pain, or symptoms that are quickly worsening or interfering with basic activities. When in doubt, contact a medical professional who can assess your specific symptoms; do not use an RPT appointment to rule out an emergency.
