## How to avoid lower back pain when running

Running is simple, but it is repetitive. Thousands of foot strikes send force up through your legs, hips, pelvis, and lower back. When your body is prepared for that load, you may barely notice it. When mileage, pace, or hills rise faster than your tissues can adapt, the lower back can become irritated.

Cleveland Clinic physical therapist Justin Nessel notes in [Cleveland Clinic's guide to lower back pain when running](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/lower-back-pain-when-running) that repetitive impact on muscles, joints, and discs is a common reason runners develop pain, and that hips and nearby areas can refer pain to the lower back. Many beginners ask the same question online, as in this [beginner runner thread about back and knee pain](https://www.reddit.com/r/beginnerrunning/comments/1iz62zl/why_do_my_back_and_knees_hurt_when_i_run_will_it/): is it normal, and will it go away? Often the answer involves how quickly training increased and how well the body was prepared.

This guide explains what a low back flare-up looks like, what symptoms runners commonly notice, how to reduce the chance of repeat flare-ups, and how RPT Clinic fits in.

## What is a low back flare-up?

A low back flare-up is a period when pain, tightness, or stiffness in the lower back increases—often after activity, a change in routine, or a long period in one position. It may last a few days or linger longer.

A flare-up does not by itself show that something is seriously damaged. It does show that the demand on your back temporarily exceeded what it was ready to handle. Learn more in our guide to [lower back pain](/health/lower-back-pain/).

## Symptoms runners often notice

Patterns that commonly match running-related low back pain include:

- A dull ache or tightness across the lower back during or after a run.
- Stiffness when getting out of bed or standing up after sitting the day after a long run.
- Pain that builds with mileage and eases with rest.
- Tight hips, hamstrings, or glutes alongside back soreness.
- Discomfort on hills, at faster paces, or late in a run when you are fatigued.
- Soreness on one side more than the other.

Pain that travels down the leg, numbness, tingling, or weakness should be assessed rather than trained through. See [when to see a doctor for back pain](/health/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-back-pain/).

## Why runners get lower back pain

Several contributors can overlap:

- **Training load.** Adding distance, speed, or hills too quickly is a common pattern, especially for beginners.
- **Hip and glute strength.** When the muscles around the hips tire, other areas may absorb more load.
- **Tight or overworked muscles.** Short, guarded muscles in the hips and trunk can limit how freely the pelvis and spine move.
- **Recovery.** Back-to-back hard sessions without rest can leave muscles fatigued.
- **Sitting time.** Long workdays can leave hips stiff before you ever lace up. See [back pain from sitting](/health/how-to-relieve-back-pain-from-sitting/).
- **Surface and footwear.** Hard surfaces and worn shoes may add to the load, though individual responses vary.

## How to avoid lower back pain when running: practical steps

1. **Build gradually.** Beginners can alternate running and walking intervals, then lengthen the running portions over several weeks. Experienced runners can avoid sudden jumps in weekly volume.
2. **Stop when pain starts.** Pushing through sharp or worsening back pain can turn a small irritation into a longer setback.
3. **Warm up.** Spend a few minutes on easy walking and gentle hip and trunk movement before you pick up pace.
4. **Strengthen the supports.** Hips, glutes, quads, calves, and trunk all absorb impact. Two short strength sessions per week are a reasonable starting point for many people.
5. **Schedule recovery.** Include easy days and full rest days, and sleep enough to recover.
6. **Cross-train when needed.** Cycling, swimming, or brisk walking may maintain fitness while your back settles.
7. **Track your pattern.** Note mileage, surface, shoes, and symptoms. Patterns often show what is driving your flare-ups.
8. **Get assessed if it keeps returning.** Repeated episodes deserve a closer look. Read [why back pain keeps coming back](/health/why-does-my-back-pain-keep-coming-back/).
