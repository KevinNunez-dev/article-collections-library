## When should you see a doctor for sciatic nerve pain?

See a healthcare professional when sciatic nerve pain is getting worse, is severe, or is not improving with self-care. You should also arrange an evaluation when symptoms interfere with sleep, walking, work, or other everyday activities.

Do not wait for a routine appointment if you develop sudden leg weakness, new bladder or bowel problems, or numbness around the groin or buttocks. These symptoms need immediate medical attention.

[Mayo Clinic’s sciatica guidance](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/sciatica/symptoms-causes/syc-20377435) recommends contacting a primary care professional when self-care does not help, or when pain lasts longer than a week, is severe, or worsens. That is a reason to check in—not a reason to wait a week when serious symptoms appear.

## Recognize the symptoms that should not wait

Seek emergency medical care for:

- New difficulty starting urination, inability to urinate, or loss of bladder control.
- New loss of bowel control or loss of awareness that you need a bowel movement.
- Numbness around the genitals, anus, or inner thighs.
- Sudden leg numbness or muscle weakness.
- Severe or worsening weakness or numbness in both legs.
- Sciatic symptoms following a serious injury, such as a traffic accident.

These signs can indicate a serious problem affecting the nerves. Do not try to manage them with stretching, massage, or a routine therapy appointment.

The [NHS sciatica guide](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sciatica/) explains the importance of immediate hospital assessment for bladder and bowel changes, saddle-area numbness, and severe or worsening symptoms affecting both legs.

## Book an evaluation when pain keeps interrupting normal life

Symptoms do not have to become an emergency before they deserve attention.

Consider how the pain affects your day:

- Are you waking up because your back or leg hurts?
- Is driving or sitting through a workday becoming difficult?
- Have you stopped walking, exercising, or doing normal household tasks?
- Do symptoms improve briefly, then return after ordinary activity?
- Are you repeatedly looking for pain relief without understanding the cause?

Tell the clinician what you can no longer do comfortably. “I can sit for only 15 minutes before pain travels into my calf” gives more useful context than a pain score alone.

## What if the pain comes and goes?

A better day is encouraging, but it does not erase a recurring pattern.

If daily back pain or repeated leg symptoms keep changing your routine, arrange an assessment rather than judging the problem only by how you feel on the appointment day.

Keep a simple record of:

- Where symptoms begin.
- How far they travel.
- Whether you notice tingling, numbness, or weakness.
- Which activities make them better or worse.
- Whether your overall function is improving.

You do not need an elaborate symptom diary. A few notes on your phone can help explain a pattern that is difficult to recreate in the examination room.

If you already have a care plan, tell your provider when symptoms change or progress stalls. Do not assume that doing more of the same exercise or treatment is always the right next step.

## Are weak back muscles causing the problem?

It is easy to blame weak back muscles when the low back feels tired, stiff, or unreliable. However, that explanation alone does not establish why sciatic nerve pain is happening.

Sciatica can involve irritation or compression of nerve roots, and similar symptoms can have different causes. An assessment should consider your history, strength, sensation, and movement—not simply assume every case needs more stretching or strengthening.

Ask the clinician:

- What findings help explain my symptoms?
- Is there evidence of a change in nerve function?
- Which activities are appropriate for me right now?
- What changes would mean I should seek care sooner?

Understanding the reason for a recommendation is more useful than collecting exercises without a clear plan.

## Should you start with primary care or a back pain specialist?

For stable, non-emergency symptoms, a primary care professional can be a starting point. They can review your medical history, examine your symptoms, and discuss whether further evaluation or referral is needed.

If you are comparing a back pain specialist or a rehabilitation clinic, ask about the provider’s qualifications, assessment process, and referral arrangements.

An appropriate provider should explain what they can assess and treat, what falls outside their scope, and which symptoms should go directly to a physician or emergency department.

## Where RPT Clinic and the RX2600 fit

For non-emergency back and leg symptoms, RPT Clinic uses an assessment-led approach to consider movement and soft-tissue contributors.

When appropriate, Robotic Precision Therapy may include the RX2600 Therapeutic Robot, which applies clinician-guided pressure to selected soft tissues. The purpose is to address identified muscular contributors as part of an individualized plan—not to diagnose the source of sciatica or promise that nerve compression has been corrected.

This treatment does not replace medical evaluation for new weakness, serious neurological symptoms, or an underlying condition that needs another type of care.

An assessment should help clarify whether RPT is suitable and when another healthcare provider needs to be involved.

## What to bring to your appointment

Bring a short symptom timeline, your medication list, and details of previous treatment or imaging.

Be ready to explain:

- When the pain started and whether an injury preceded it.
- Whether symptoms stay in the back or travel into the leg.
- Any changes in sensation, strength, walking, or balance.
- What you have tried and how you responded.
- What you most want to get back to doing.

The goal is not just temporary pain relief. It is to understand the next appropriate step, establish a plan, and know which changes require reassessment.
