## When should you see a doctor for back pain?

See a healthcare professional when back pain is not improving, keeps getting worse, or interferes with sleep, work, walking, or other everyday activities. You do not need to wait until you can barely move.

If a mild episode is improving, comfortable activity and monitoring may be reasonable. However, persistent symptoms, an unexplained change in your usual pain, or new symptoms deserve attention.

The [National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/back-pain) recommends seeing a doctor when pain does not improve after a few weeks or occurs with symptoms such as weakness, fever, or unintended weight loss.

## When back pain needs emergency care

Go to an emergency department for back pain with:

- New difficulty urinating or loss of bladder or bowel control.
- Loss of feeling around the genitals, anus, or inner thighs.
- New pain, tingling, weakness, or numbness affecting both legs.
- Chest pain.
- Symptoms following a serious accident.

Do not wait for a routine therapy visit or try to stretch through these symptoms.

Seek prompt medical advice for sudden severe pain, rapidly worsening pain, fever, or feeling generally unwell. The [NHS back pain guide](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/) separates these urgent concerns from symptoms that can be assessed through a scheduled appointment.

## When to book a non-emergency appointment

Consider an evaluation when you notice:

- Daily back pain that keeps limiting your routine.
- Pain that has not improved after a few weeks.
- Difficulty sleeping or pain that is worse at night.
- Unexplained weight loss.
- Pain that repeatedly returns after ordinary activity.
- New tingling, numbness, or symptoms traveling into a leg.

A clinician can decide whether you need further investigation, rehabilitation, medical treatment, or a combination.

If symptoms travel from the low back into the buttock or leg, read [when to see a doctor for sciatic nerve pain](/health/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-sciatic-nerve-pain/). Sciatica is one possible symptom pattern, not an explanation for every painful back.

## Do not assume it is just weak back muscles

You may suspect weak back muscles, poor posture, or too much sitting. Those details can be relevant, but they do not establish a diagnosis.

Tell the clinician what actually happens: “My back hurts after ten minutes of standing” or “I wake up when I turn over.” Specific examples help more than guessing which muscle needs fixing.

If relief is temporary and the same pattern keeps returning, our guide to [why back pain keeps coming back](/health/why-does-my-back-pain-keep-coming-back/) explains useful questions to bring to an assessment.

## What to ask a back pain specialist

Whether you start with primary care or consult a back pain specialist, ask:

- What findings help explain my symptoms?
- Are there warning signs that need another type of evaluation?
- What can I continue doing safely?
- How will we measure progress?
- When should the plan change if I am not improving?

A useful plan should explain both the treatment and the circumstances that would trigger reassessment.
