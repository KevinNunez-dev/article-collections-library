## Understanding Disc Anatomy

Your spine consists of vertebrae (bones) separated by intervertebral discs. Each disc has:

- **Outer layer (annulus fibrosus)** — Tough, fibrous ring that holds the disc together
- **Inner core (nucleus pulposus)** — Soft, gel-like center that absorbs shock and distributes forces

These discs act as shock absorbers, allowing movement while protecting your spinal cord and nerves.

## What is a Herniated Disc?

A herniated (also called "slipped" or "ruptured") disc occurs when disc material extends through a weakened area in the outer layer. The disc material can irritate or compress nearby nerve roots, causing pain, numbness, weakness, or tingling.

**Important distinction:** Not all herniated discs cause pain. Many people have herniated discs on imaging with zero symptoms. The pain depends on whether and how the disc irritates nearby nerves.

## Common Herniated Disc Symptoms

- **Neck or back pain** (localized to the disc location)
- **Pain traveling into an arm** (cervical/upper disc herniation)
- **Pain traveling into buttock or leg** (lumbar/lower disc herniation)
- **Tingling, numbness, or burning** sensations
- **Weakness** in affected arm or leg
- **Difficulty with coordination or walking**
- **Loss of bladder or bowel control** (rare emergency symptom)

## What Causes Disc Herniation?

Disc herniation can occur from:

### Acute Injury or Trauma
A sudden impact, heavy lift, or fall can cause immediate disc rupture.

### Repetitive Loading & Microtrauma
Years of poor posture, sedentary habits, and repeated bending create gradual disc wear. The annulus (outer layer) develops small tears over time.

### Degenerative Disc Disease
As discs naturally age, they lose water content and structural integrity. They become more prone to rupture.

### Muscle Imbalance & Spine Misalignment
**This is the key factor most practitioners overlook.** When surrounding muscles become tight and imbalanced, they pull the spine out of alignment. This creates uneven load distribution on discs and vertebrae, accelerating degeneration and creating conditions for herniation.

A contracted psoas, tight erector spinae, or imbalanced core muscles pull vertebrae closer together, compressing discs and increasing herniation risk.

## The Critical Discovery: Muscle Imbalance Amplifies Disc Problems

While structural disc herniation shows on MRI, **most herniated disc pain actually comes from two things**:

1. **Surrounding muscles in protective spasm** — When nerves are irritated, muscles tighten to protect the area. This tightness becomes chronic.
2. **Spine misalignment from muscle imbalance** — Tight muscles pull vertebrae into compression, intensifying pressure on the herniated disc and irritating nerves further.

**Traditional surgery addresses the disc itself, but doesn't fix the muscle imbalance that created the problem in the first place. This is why many patients have recurrent pain after disc surgery.**

## The Pain Cycle of Herniated Discs

1. Disc herniates and irritates a nerve
2. Pain and inflammation cause protective muscle guarding
3. Muscles tighten to "splint" the area
4. Tight muscles pull spine into compression
5. Compression intensifies disc irritation
6. Cycle perpetuates—pain → more tightness → worse compression → more pain

**RPT breaks this cycle by addressing the muscle component.**

## L4-L5 and L5-S1 Disc Herniation: Most Common Sites

The two most common levels for lumbar disc herniation are:

**L4-L5 (between 4th and 5th lumbar vertebrae)**
- Pain often radiates into buttock and lateral leg
- Weakness or numbness can affect foot

**L5-S1 (between 5th lumbar and sacrum)**
- Pain radiates into buttock, back of leg, calf
- Often causes sciatica-like symptoms
- Can affect foot sensation and strength

RPT is particularly effective for these levels, as the robot can precisely target the psoas and other deep muscles creating compression at these sites.
