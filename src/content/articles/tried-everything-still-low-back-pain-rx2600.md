## Still have low back pain after trying different treatments?

Low back pain can make ordinary tasks—getting out of a car, sitting through a meeting, lifting groceries, or sleeping comfortably—feel like a project. If you have tried stretching, physical therapy, massage, chiropractic care, medication, or rest without lasting improvement, it is natural to wonder whether there is another option.

The next step is not automatically more treatment. Persistent pain is a reason to revisit what has been assessed, what each treatment was intended to change, and whether your symptoms call for another medical opinion. RPT Clinic in Troy, Michigan, offers Robotic Precision Therapy (RPT), which uses the RX2600 Therapeutic Robot to apply controlled, repeatable pressure to selected muscles under clinician direction. This may be an option to discuss when muscular contributors appear relevant. It is not a diagnosis, a guaranteed solution, or a replacement for appropriate medical care.

## Why low back pain can persist

“Low back pain” describes a symptom in an area, not one specific condition. Symptoms may relate to muscles, joints, discs, nerves, tendons, prior injury, activity demands, or a combination. Pain can also change the way a person moves: guarding an area may make everyday movement feel stiffer, while doing much less can reduce tolerance for normal tasks. These patterns can overlap, so a sore muscle does not prove that the muscle started the problem.

Sometimes the first treatment plan needs adjusting. The original goal may have been pain relief, but the next useful goal could be sitting longer, walking comfortably, returning to work, or increasing strength. In other cases, the diagnosis or safety concerns need more attention before selecting another hands-on treatment. An imaging finding, when present, also needs to be interpreted alongside symptoms and an examination; a scan alone does not determine the best plan.

Think of a treatment that “didn’t work” as information. Did it provide temporary relief? Did your movement improve even if pain remained? Did an exercise increase symptoms? Did anything change over several weeks? Sharing these details can help a clinician decide whether to modify the plan, investigate another cause, or consider a different approach.

## What should be reassessed?

An evaluation should look beyond the pain score. Be ready to describe when symptoms began, whether there was an injury, what makes them better or worse, whether pain travels into a leg, and how your normal activities have changed. Mention numbness, tingling, weakness, fever, unexplained weight changes, recent procedures, and relevant health conditions. Those details can affect what kind of care is appropriate.

You can ask the clinician:

- What causes are you considering, and what findings support that explanation?
- Does anything suggest a nerve or another issue that needs medical evaluation?
- What activity or function should improve if this plan is helping?
- How long should I try the plan before we review my progress?
- What symptoms mean I should stop and contact a clinician sooner?

These are reasonable questions whether you are seeing a physician, physical therapist, chiropractor, or another qualified provider. A good plan should explain what it is meant to address and what it cannot address.

## Comparing common low-back-pain options

Physical therapy is a broad approach that may combine education, movement practice, strengthening, mobility work, and ways to build tolerance for daily tasks. If you have tried PT before, ask what the goals were and whether the plan can be adjusted to your current symptoms and schedule. A prior unsuccessful course does not establish that all physical therapy is unsuitable.

Massage and personal tools may help some people feel more comfortable for a time, but temporary relief does not identify the source of pain. Avoid repeatedly applying intense pressure to an area that is swollen, injured, numb, or sharply painful. Chiropractic care may include spinal manipulation; whether that is suitable depends on the person’s symptoms and clinical assessment. Ask about expected benefits, risks, alternatives, and what to do if symptoms worsen.

Medication decisions should be made with a clinician or pharmacist who can consider your medical history and other medicines. The American College of Physicians guideline includes several non-drug options for some types of low back pain, but it does not establish that one treatment is best for every person. Nor does general low-back-pain guidance prove that a particular proprietary device is effective.

## A simple way to track progress

Choose two or three activities that matter to you: putting on shoes, walking the dog, sitting at work, or sleeping through the night. Before and after a treatment plan, note how long you can do each activity and whether symptoms change later that day or the next morning. A daily log does not need to be complicated; brief notes are enough.

Pain can fluctuate, so one unusually good or bad day does not tell the whole story. Look at the pattern over time and share it with your clinician. If function is not moving in the direction you hoped, ask whether the plan should be modified or whether you need a different assessment. There is no need to continue a costly or uncomfortable approach simply because you already started it.

## If you are considering another opinion

A second opinion can be useful when the diagnosis is unclear, symptoms have changed, or a proposed treatment feels like a major decision. It does not mean you have to reject the first clinician’s advice. It is an opportunity to check that the explanation fits your symptoms and that you understand the available choices.

Bring the information you already have instead of starting from memory. A short timeline, medication list, prior imaging reports, and notes about treatments can prevent repeating the same questions. Describe your main limitation in everyday terms: “I can walk for ten minutes before I need to sit,” for example. Ask whether any test or referral is actually likely to change the plan; more testing is not automatically more useful.

When comparing clinics, look for a clear evaluation process, a provider who invites questions, and realistic language about outcomes. Ask whether the provider will coordinate with your physician if your symptoms suggest another cause. Find out the total expected cost, how follow-up is decided, and whether you can stop or change course. Be wary of pressure to purchase a large package before anyone has assessed you or of claims that a treatment can identify and permanently fix the “one root cause” of all back pain.

If you are choosing between treatments, compare what they aim to do. Exercise and movement-based care may build capacity; massage or pressure-based approaches may focus on comfort or soft tissue; medical evaluation may be necessary to assess a diagnosis. These goals may overlap, but they are not proof that the treatments are interchangeable. Read the clinic’s [comparison of physical therapy and RPT](/health/physical-therapy-vs-robotic-precision-therapy-lower-back-pain) and use your clinician’s guidance to decide which questions matter to you.

## When low back pain needs urgent care

Seek emergency medical care for new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the groin or saddle area, or rapidly worsening weakness in a leg. Promptly contact a medical professional for severe pain after major trauma, fever with back pain, or symptoms that are quickly worsening. Do not book a therapy session to rule out an emergency.
